What counseling after divorce actually involves

The American Psychological Association’s dictionary describes divorce counseling as counseling for individuals and their family members to help them cope with the problems that result from divorce. Family members may be encouraged to let go of the past and deal with present emotions. Spouses may look at their own part in the breakup so that blame decreases (APA Dictionary of Psychology).

In practice, that usually means a few things happening over several weeks or months:

  • Treating the divorce as a real loss. The American Counseling Association’s magazine describes therapists helping clients accept divorce as a major loss, often with feelings of betrayal and trauma (Counseling Today).
  • Working through anger, shame and blame. Some therapists use emotion-focused exercises, such as speaking aloud to an imagined version of your ex from both the angry and the hurt side.
  • Learning to say what you need. That includes setting healthy boundaries with an ex-partner who is still in your life through children, family or shared friends.
  • Rebuilding. The same article describes a grief model that ends in a rebuilding stage, when acceptance deepens and a person finds a way forward.

A counselor can also challenge a story you have been telling yourself. The article notes one counselor’s observation that many women want to take all the responsibility for a relationship ending, and that she helps them see that both partners played a role. The same pattern happens in reverse: some people place all the blame on themselves, others on the other person. A neutral third party helps you see the whole picture.

Is it time to start? Signs that counseling would help

Sadness after a divorce is expected. Myra Wilkinson, PhD, LMFT-S, told the American Counseling Association’s November 2026 magazine that grief, depression, anger and denial are common in divorce. The concern, she said, is when those feelings interrupt normal routines.

That gives you a practical test. Consider starting if:

  • You are sleeping badly, or sleeping most of the day, for weeks.
  • Work, housework or caring for your kids is slipping.
  • You replay the marriage constantly and cannot move toward a decision, a conversation or a plan.
  • You feel numb, ashamed or hopeless.
  • Conflict with your ex keeps flaring up and you cannot calm it down.
  • You are drinking more than you want to, or relying on other substances to get through the day.

If you cannot sleep, eat or work for days, feel hopeless, are using alcohol or drugs to cope, or cannot care for yourself or your children, talk to a doctor or licensed therapist soon. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 right now. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day anywhere in the United States.

It is not too late, and it is not too early

People search for this topic at every stage: mid-separation, the week the decree is signed, or three years later when an old wound reopens. Therapy works at each of those points. Early on, the focus is stabilizing. Later, it is identity, trust and what you want your next chapter to look like.

Co-parenting after divorce: where counseling helps

If you share children with your ex, you are not finished with each other, and that makes co-parenting one of the hardest parts of recovery. The American Psychological Association notes that the end of a marriage typically brings a flood of emotions, including anger, grief, anxiety and fear, and that parents can do a lot during a divorce to ease their child’s transition.

When classes and good intentions are not enough

The American Bar Association’s Family Advocate says to consider professional help when general solutions, such as coparenting classes, are not working or are hard to put in place. Examples include rules for the children that are hard to establish, a child who resents a stepparent, or a sudden rise in conflict between the biological parents (ABA Family Advocate, 2023). The same article warns that children who do not develop healthy coping skills are at serious risk of later psychological problems.

In Texas, courts commonly require divorcing parents to complete a parenting education course. Counseling goes further. It helps you handle the specific friction in your own family: handoffs, discipline that differs between homes, holidays, and how to speak about the other parent in front of the kids.

What the counseling literature says about the children

In the November 2026 issue of Counseling Today, a counselor frames the core question for parents in high-conflict divorce as what you are showing your children about how you care for them and for each other through this process. When children see parents working together toward their wellbeing, that is a win. The magazine also notes there is no official definition of high-conflict divorce, though counselors can often sense it (Counseling Today, November 2026).

What to do between sessions

Therapy happens an hour at a time, and recovery happens in the other 167 hours of the week. Wilkinson teaches clients what she calls the 5 Bs of Self-Care: Breath, Boundaries, Belonging, Belief and Balance, starting with intentional breathing to regulate emotions (Counseling Today, November 2026). Here is how that can look in daily life:

  1. Breath. Before you read a text from your ex or walk into a handoff, slow your breathing for a minute.
  2. Boundaries. Decide which topics you will discuss with your ex (the children’s schedule) and which you will not.
  3. Belonging. Keep one standing weekly connection with a friend, a small group or a congregation.
  4. Belief. Return to the beliefs and practices that steady you, whether prayer, scripture or other routines.
  5. Balance. Protect sleep, meals and movement, even when you do not feel like it.

Parents often feel guilty about taking time for themselves. The same article quotes the counselor’s reminder that your kids need to see you healthy.

How to choose a therapist after divorce

A good fit matters more than a long list of credentials. The American Psychological Association suggests deciding up front whether you want therapy alone, with a partner, or with your children, and what your main goals are. It also suggests thinking about whether you will use health insurance or an employee assistance program, and how much you can afford out of pocket (APA, Understanding psychotherapy). It advises a short phone conversation with a possible therapist about training, clinical expertise, experience with problems like yours, fees, sliding-scale policy, insurance and missed-appointment policy.

For families, the ABA article adds that a qualified family therapist should know child development and research on separated families, understand the legal framework of divorce, and stay professionally objective between households. It also says a therapist should not make recommendations about parenting plans or give opinions about family members they have not met (ABA Family Advocate, 2023). That is a good standard to hold any counselor to.

A quick checklist for your first call:

Question Why it matters
Do you work with people after divorce? Experience with this specific transition
Will you see me alone, my children, or both of us? Matches the format to your goals
Do you take my insurance, or what are your fees? Prevents billing surprises
Do you offer telehealth? Helpful if you have no childcare or live outside Tyler
Can my faith be part of the work? Important if belief is part of your recovery

FAQ

How long does it take to heal after a divorce?

There is no fixed timeline, and the grief models counselors use describe stages that people move through at different speeds and sometimes revisit. A better measure than the calendar is whether your sleep, work, parenting and relationships are steadier than they were. Counseling gives you tools to move that process along.

Is therapy after divorce only for people who are struggling badly?

No. You can start when you notice that grief, anger or worry are interrupting your routines, long before a crisis. Many people also come in after the legal process ends, once the adrenaline fades and the loss sinks in.

Can my children see a counselor, too?

Yes. Children and teens often hold back at home to protect their parents, so a separate counselor gives them space to talk. At The Bridge Therapeutic Services, child and teen counseling and parenting support can run alongside your own sessions.

Does insurance cover counseling after divorce?

Many health plans cover outpatient counseling, but coverage varies by plan. The Bridge Therapeutic Services accepts private pay and health insurance, so you can ask about your options when you reach out.
