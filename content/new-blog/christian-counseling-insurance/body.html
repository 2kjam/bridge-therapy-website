Does health insurance cover Christian counseling?

Three things decide it.

  1. The provider. Plans pay licensed professionals, such as licensed professional counselors, clinical social workers, marriage and family therapists and psychologists. A licensed therapist who also holds Christian beliefs and invites faith into the work is still a licensed therapist.
  2. The reason for the visit. Plans generally pay for services that are medically or psychologically necessary for a mental health condition, such as anxiety, depression, trauma or grief that has become a clinical problem. The TRICARE manual, for example, pays only for services necessary for a mental disorder.
  3. The network. A network is a group of providers that have an agreement with your health plan. Plans may call these providers “preferred” or “participating,” and using one outside the network may cost you more or not be covered at all.

Notice what is not on the list: the word “Christian.” Insurers do not usually pay for a service because it is faith-based, and they do not usually refuse it for that reason either. They look at the credential, the diagnosis and the plan.

Licensed therapy versus pastoral or biblical counseling

People searching for Christian counseling often mean one of two different services, and insurance treats them differently.

Licensed counseling that integrates faith. A state-licensed therapist uses established psychological methods and also welcomes prayer, Scripture or spiritual questions when the client wants them. This is the type of care that health plans are built to reimburse, provided the plan and network rules are met.

Pastoral or biblical counseling. This is guidance grounded in ministry and Scripture, often offered through churches. It is valuable for many people, but it is a separate category. Medicare Part B covers certain outpatient mental health services, including family counseling when its main purpose is to help with your treatment. Ask Medicare or your provider whether your specific service is covered. See Medicare’s outpatient mental health coverage.

Since January 1, 2024, eligible Medicare-enrolled marriage and family therapists can bill Medicare independently for the diagnosis and treatment of mental illness. CMS explains the eligibility and enrollment requirements. These are Medicare’s coverage and billing rules, not confirmation that The Bridge or any particular therapist accepts Medicare.

The TRICARE manual shows the picture is not identical across programs: it lists pastoral counselors among qualified mental health providers, but only for services that are necessary for a mental disorder. Rules vary by program, so the plan you hold is the authority.

What mental health parity means for you

The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) may apply to many private health plans and certain Medicaid and CHIP plans. Where it applies, mental health and substance use disorder benefits must meet parity requirements relative to medical and surgical benefits. The U.S. Department of Labor explains the protections for cost sharing and treatment limits. Federal MHPAEA parity rules do not apply to Medicare; Medicare has its own coverage rules.

For plans subject to MHPAEA, federal guidance gives two examples of what a plan may not do:

  • Limit mental health care to 20 outpatient visits a year when no such limit applies to medical and surgical care.
  • Charge a separate $250 mental health deductible on top of a $250 medical deductible in the same category.

Both examples come from the federal self-compliance tool for MHPAEA and ERISA requirements. If you think your plan is applying a limit like these, ask the plan to explain it in writing.

How to find a Christian counselor in your network

Directories are a starting point, not a guarantee. A reliable routine looks like this:

  1. Call your plan or check its directory. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) tells members to call the health plan or state Medicaid or CHIP office, or use the plan’s website or member handbook, and to confirm that the information is current.
  2. Narrow by what you need. Look for licensed counselors who treat your concern, whether that is anxiety, grief, trauma, parenting or marriage.
  3. Confirm with the counselor’s office. When you make the appointment, give your Member ID and group number from your card and ask whether the office accepts your plan. Networks change, so the CMS guidance says to re-check at scheduling.
  4. Keep a note of who you spoke with and when. If a bill surprises you later, the date and name help.

Directories are often wrong, which is why step 3 matters. A New York Attorney General report notes that the No Surprises Act, effective January 1, 2022, requires private plans to keep accurate online directories, verify them at least every 90 days and apply in-network cost sharing when a provider was wrongly listed as in network. That is a protection worth knowing, though it is best to avoid the problem by confirming before the first session.

Questions to ask your insurer and your counselor

Counseling falls under “behavioral health,” which can have its own rules, reimbursement and procedures separate from your medical benefits, as Northwestern University’s counseling center explains. Call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask:

  • Does my plan cover outpatient counseling, and at what rate?
  • What is my copay or coinsurance per session, and how much of my deductible is left?
  • How many visits are covered per year?
  • Do I need a referral from a doctor, or prior authorization?
  • Which providers are preferred or in network near me?
  • Is telehealth covered, and does it cost the same as an office visit?
  • Are free case managers available? SAMHSA says this is worth asking.

When you contact the counselor’s office, the CMS roadmap suggests asking whether the provider is accepting new patients, accepts your insurance, what you may pay out of pocket, what conditions they treat and whether telehealth is offered.

For a child or teen, call the plan using the policyholder’s information and ask whether dependents have the same behavioral health benefits. For couples or family counseling, ask how the plan handles sessions where the diagnosis belongs to one person, because plans often need a diagnosis for one member of the family.

What you may owe: copays, deductibles and coinsurance

Insurance does not mean free. What you pay per session depends on your plan design:

Cost typeWhat it meansWhen you pay it
CopayA flat fee per visitAt each session
DeductibleAn amount you pay before the plan starts sharing costsEarly in the plan year
CoinsuranceA percentage of the session cost after the deductibleAt each session or on a later bill
Out-of-pocket maximumThe most you pay in a plan year for covered careUntil the limit is reached

To show how widely this varies, the New York State of Health’s 2026 standard plan chart lists office-visit copays such as $15 for Platinum plans, $30 for the first outpatient mental health visit on Silver plans and $50 for the first three visits on Bronze plans. The chart also says mental health cost sharing may be lower than listed to comply with parity. Those are New York examples, not Texas figures, and your own plan documents set your amount.

SAMHSA notes that Medicare covers most treatment costs in most cases, while Medicaid and CHIP cover some mental health treatment, with amounts and services depending on the state. For Texas families on Medicaid or CHIP, the state program’s own member services line can confirm what applies.

When a counselor is out of network

Some plans include out-of-network benefits. In that case, Northwestern University explains, members typically pay in full at each visit, ask the provider for a paid invoice called a superbill, send it to the insurer’s claims department and receive a reimbursement for part of the session cost. Out-of-network benefits usually come with higher deductibles and higher copays or coinsurance.

Before choosing this route, ask your plan three things: whether you have out-of-network mental health benefits, what the reimbursement percentage is, and how long you have to submit claims.

Telehealth counseling and insurance

Many families in Texas use video sessions because of distance, schedules or a child’s comfort at home. Coverage rules for telehealth differ by plan, so ask whether your plan covers video counseling and whether the cost matches an office visit. Billing details also matter on the provider side: claims must be coded correctly, and guidance on telehealth place of service billing explains why a wrong code can lead to denied claims.

FAQ

Do Christian counselors take insurance?

Many do, but it depends on the practice and the plan. Licensed Christian counselors can bill insurance like any other licensed therapist, while pastoral and ministry-based counselors often do not. The Bridge Therapeutic Services bills private pay or health insurance, and you can contact us to confirm how your plan works.

Will my insurer know the counseling is Christian?

Your insurer receives billing information such as the provider, date, service code and diagnosis, which relate to the clinical care. The faith-informed approach is part of how the counselor works with you, not a billing category.

How many counseling sessions does insurance cover?

It varies by plan. Some plans set a yearly visit limit, and where parity rules apply, a mental health limit cannot be more restrictive than limits on comparable medical and surgical benefits. Call the number on your card and ask how many visits are covered per year and whether any need prior authorization.

Does insurance cover couples or family counseling?

Coverage differs by plan and often depends on whether the sessions address a diagnosed condition for one family member. Medicare Part B covers certain outpatient mental health services, including family counseling when its main purpose is to help with your treatment. Ask Medicare or your provider whether your specific service is covered. Since January 1, 2024, eligible Medicare-enrolled marriage and family therapists can bill Medicare independently for the diagnosis and treatment of mental illness. See the Medicare coverage details and CMS provider billing requirements.

What if I cannot use insurance?

You can pay privately for sessions. If your plan has out-of-network benefits, you may be able to submit a superbill for partial reimbursement. Our guide to therapy costs in Texas explains what to expect.
