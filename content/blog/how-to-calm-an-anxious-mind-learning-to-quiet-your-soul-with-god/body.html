There is a little three-verse Psalm I keep coming back to: Psalm 131.

It is short enough to read in less than a minute, yet there is something remarkably comforting about it. David writes:

“I have calmed and quieted my soul.”

I find myself wondering what was happening around David when he wrote those words.

His life certainly wasn’t calm. He knew pressure, responsibility, conflict, uncertainty, fear, disappointment, and waiting. He knew what it felt like to be pursued, misunderstood, and surrounded by circumstances he couldn’t control.

And yet, he says, “I have calmed and quieted my soul.”

He doesn’t say, My circumstances became calm.

He doesn’t say, I figured everything out.

He doesn’t say, I finally got control of everything that was making me anxious.

He says, “I have calmed and quieted my soul.”

We Can Practice a Different Response to Anxiety

Our brains are shaped by repetition.

The thoughts, behaviors, and responses we practice repeatedly can become increasingly familiar and automatic. This is one reason patterns of worry, rumination, overthinking, and anxiety can become so difficult to interrupt—and why practicing a different response can matter.

When something feels uncertain, our minds naturally want to solve it.

What if this happens?

What does that mean?

What should I do?

How can I make sure this doesn’t happen again?

What am I missing?

The mind keeps reaching for certainty because it believes certainty will finally allow us to feel safe.

But sometimes the search for certainty becomes the very thing keeping us activated.

Psalm 131 gives us another way.

Instead of continually reaching for what is too big for us, David describes bringing himself back to a quieter place with God.

This is not pretending that life is easy.

It is learning where to place what we cannot carry.

“God, I Trust You With This.”

I can imagine David reminding himself:

This is too big for me.

God, I trust You with this.

God, You are big. It is okay for me to be small here.

I don’t have to have all the answers today.

I don’t “got this,” but You do.

There is something meaningful about this practice.

When we are anxious, our attention can become consumed by the problem. We scan, analyze, predict, rehearse, and attempt to control.

But we can learn to notice what is happening inside us and intentionally redirect our attention toward what is true.

Not through denial.

Not through pretending we aren’t afraid.

Not through forcing ourselves to “just calm down.”

Instead, we practice returning.

Again and again and again.

Calming Your Soul Is a Practice

David says, “I have calmed and quieted my soul.”

That language suggests intentionality.

He didn’t simply wait until peace happened to him.

He practiced a different posture.

He stopped reaching for what was too big for him.

He remembered who God was.

He accepted his own limitations.

He released the demand to understand everything.

And he returned to God.

This is important because emotional regulation isn’t about never becoming anxious.

A regulated person can still experience fear, uncertainty, sadness, anger, or stress.

The difference is that those emotions don’t have to become the place where we live.

We can learn to notice:

My thoughts are racing.

I’m trying to solve something I cannot solve right now.

I’m looking for certainty.

My body is tense.

I’m carrying something that belongs to God.

And then we can pause.

Breathe.

Pray.

Tell ourselves the truth.

And return.

Your Nervous System Can Learn a New Place to Land

When we repeatedly practice slowing down and turning toward what is true, we are practicing a different response to stress.

Over time, the body and mind can become more familiar with slowing down rather than immediately escalating into worry and overthinking.

You may notice your breathing soften.

Your shoulders drop.

Your jaw relax.

Your heart rate settle.

Your thoughts become less scattered.

You become more able to distinguish between what you can control and what you need to surrender.

And perhaps most importantly, you begin to learn:

I don’t have to solve everything in this moment.

That is a very different message from the anxious mind.

The anxious mind says:

Figure it out.

Fix it.

Make sure.

Know.

Control.

Faith can teach us to say:

I can be here without having all the answers.

God is here.

I can trust Him with what I cannot control.

Peace Doesn’t Come From Having All the Answers

One of the great traps of anxiety is believing that if we could just figure everything out, we would finally be at peace.

But there will always be something we don’t know.

Something we can’t control.

Something we can’t predict.

Something we cannot fix.

Peace, then, cannot ultimately depend on having complete certainty.

Psalm 131 points us somewhere else.

It points us toward God.

Toward humility.

Toward surrender.

Toward learning to become quiet in His presence even when circumstances remain uncertain.

We don’t have to know everything.

We don’t have to carry everything.

We don’t have to understand everything today.

We can come back to what is true:

God is God.

I am not.

He is capable of carrying what I cannot.

I can rest in Him.

Practicing the Return

The next time you notice yourself spiraling into worry or overthinking, try something simple.

Pause and ask:

What am I trying to figure out right now that I may not be able to figure out?

Then take a slow breath and remind yourself:

“I don’t have to have all the answers today.”

“God, I trust You with this.”

“You are bigger than this situation.”

“I don’t have to carry what belongs to You.”

Then return your attention to the present moment.

You may have to do it again thirty seconds later.

That’s okay.

And again.

And again.

That is the practice.

We don’t practice peace because we have finally figured everything out.

We practice returning to God while we haven’t.

And over time, our souls can learn a new place to land.

“I have calmed and quieted my soul.”

Perhaps that is part of what David is teaching us in Psalm 131: sometimes the path to peace isn’t finding the answer.

Sometimes it is learning to become quiet enough to remember Who is holding the answer.
